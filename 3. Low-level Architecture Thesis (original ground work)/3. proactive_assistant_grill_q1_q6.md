# Proactive Assistant — User Journeys, Features & Technical Grilling

> Continuation of the grilling docs. This doc covers: (1) the user journeys that justify this role existing at all, (2) the product features they map to, and (3) the hard architecture questions you raised — answered with researched, current numbers, not vibes.

---

## Part A — User Journeys

The brief frames this simply — *"the agent should not wait for users to ask questions."* But the hard part isn't generating the message, it's deciding **when** to speak without becoming noise. Five journeys:

### Journey 1 — "Your score moved — want to know why?" (New bureau pull)
A new credit report lands, the score changed, and the user is anxious or curious before they've even opened the app.
**Feature it maps to:** Push notification on score-delta detection, surfacing the causal explanation as soon as the user opens the app.

### Journey 2 — "Your bill is due in 2 days." (Upcoming due date)
Routine, calendar-driven — no prediction needed, just a known due date approaching.
**Feature it maps to:** Scheduled reminder with a one-tap "set a reminder" or "pay now" action.

### Journey 3 — "Your utilization just crossed 75%." (Risk threshold breach)
A specific, bureau-data-derived threshold is crossed — not a date, a state change.
**Feature it maps to:** Threshold-triggered alert surfacing what's driving the utilization spike.

### Journey 4 — "You're about to miss this EMI — here's what's at stake." (Predicted shortfall)
This is the brief's most ambitious proactive example: predicting a cash shortfall *before* a payment is due, based on the user's bank balance trend, and suggesting restructuring preemptively.
**Feature it maps to:** Predictive shortfall alert — this is also the journey with the hardest open questions in this entire role, covered in depth below.

### Journey 5 — "Your dispute has been open 30 days — you're owed compensation." (Regulatory SLA breach)
GoodScore's own regulatory research established that the CICRA framework entitles a user to ₹100/day if a bureau dispute isn't resolved within 30 days.
**Feature it maps to:** A scheduled SLA-tracking job that fires automatically on Day 31, surfacing the compensation claim and an escalation path to the RBI Integrated Ombudsman.

**The pattern across all five:** every journey is **system-initiated** — nobody asked it anything. That single property is what makes this role categorically harder than it looks: every message it sends is an interruption, and every wrong or poorly-timed interruption has a real cost.

## Part B — The Questions, Grilled

### Q1. Journey 4 depends on knowing the user's bank balance — how does Proactive actually get that data?

This is the question worth starting with, because it's where Gemini's original research proposed something that doesn't survive contact with current platform policy.

**What Gemini's research proposed:** parsing the user's SMS messages on-device to detect transaction patterns and predict EMI shortfalls — *"by securely analyzing SMS transaction data locally on the device... the Proactive Assistant maps upcoming EMIs against predicted bank balances."*

**Why this doesn't actually work in 2026:** Google Play's current developer policy is explicit and binding — **an app must be the user's designated default handler for SMS in order to even declare the READ_SMS permission in its manifest at all.** GoodScore is a credit-management app, not an SMS app; there's no realistic path to it becoming a user's default SMS handler, and Google has a well-documented enforcement history specifically targeting Indian lending/loan apps for exactly this category of sensitive-permission misuse (action against 3,500+ loan apps on the Play Store for policy violations is publicly documented). This isn't a "be careful with consent UX" problem — it's a **Play Store distribution-eligibility problem.** An app built around SMS parsing for this purpose is at real risk of not surviving Play Store review at all, independent of how good the consent flow is.

**The legitimate alternative that already exists for exactly this data:** India's **Account Aggregator (AA) framework** — an RBI-regulated, consent-first system specifically designed to let a user share real bank balance and transaction data with a licensed Financial Information User, through a licensed Account Aggregator that acts as a "blind pipe" (it routes encrypted data but cannot read or store it). This is not a workaround — it's the **sanctioned, purpose-built mechanism** for exactly the use case Gemini's research was reaching for. It already has real adoption (252.9 million users with linked accounts as of late 2025), and lending-sector NBFCs are already its largest user category.

**Recommendation: scrap SMS parsing entirely, build Journey 4 on the Account Aggregator framework instead.** GoodScore registers as a Financial Information User (FIU), integrates with a licensed AA (e.g., Finvu, OneMoney, Anumati), and requests bank balance/transaction data through an explicit, revocable, purpose-specific consent flow — fully within RBI's own regulatory rails, not adjacent to them. This is a stronger answer than the brief's own proposal, not just a safer one: it's instant-revocable, doesn't require the app to hold raw SMS content at all, and aligns with where Indian fintech data-sharing is actually heading rather than a permission category Google is actively restricting.

### Q2. Even with AA-based bank data, is "predict EMI shortfall before it happens" actually a sound feature, or does prediction-under-uncertainty introduce a worse problem?

This is the question worth asking before building Journey 4 at all — prediction means being wrong sometimes, and in a financial product, being wrong proactively is a different risk than being wrong reactively.

**The core risk:** a false positive here ("you're about to miss your EMI") sent to someone already in a fragile financial and emotional state is not a neutral inconvenience — it's the kind of message that could trigger real anxiety in exactly the demographic GoodScore exists to serve. A false negative (missing a genuine shortfall) means the entire point of the feature silently fails at the moment it mattered most.

**Recommendation:** frame this explicitly as **directional risk-flagging, not a confident prediction** — the message itself should never claim certainty it doesn't have. Something like *"Your balance trend suggests this EMI might be tight — want to look at it together?"* rather than *"You will miss this payment."* This isn't just a copywriting choice — it changes the underlying bar: the system needs a **statistical confidence threshold below which it simply doesn't fire**, rather than always alerting on its best guess. A feature that's silent on ambiguous cases and only speaks when the signal is strong is more trustworthy than one that's always talking with false confidence.

### Q3. What actually decides *when* to fire a proactive message — is this rule-based, or does it need an LLM at all?

**Your pushback here mattered — the first pass at this answer was wrong, and it's worth showing why.**

The first version of this answer reached for an LLM on Journey 4 — calling it "directional risk-flagging" and implying it needed a model to assess confidence. You pushed back hard: *these are nudges, this looks like a plain software problem with background schedulers — why involve an LLM at all?*

You were right. Stripped down, Journey 4 is two plain steps:
- **Detect:** a trend projection on the AA-sourced balance data (moving average / simple regression) against a known EMI amount and due date — statistics, not language understanding.
- **Notify:** plug the result into a fixed sentence template — `"Your {bill_type} due {due_date} for ₹{amount} might be tight based on your recent balance"` — not a generative response.

**Corrected answer: zero LLM, for all five journeys — including Journey 4.** Every journey here is a **detect → template** pipeline:

| Journey | What detects it | Trigger shape |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Score change | Bureau pull event | Event-driven |
| 2. Bill due | Date comparison | Time-driven |
| 3. Utilization > 75% | Threshold check on bureau data | Event-driven |
| 4. Predicted EMI shortfall | Trend projection on AA balance data | Event-driven |
| 5. Dispute SLA breach | Date comparison (30 days) | Time-driven |

You also flagged that this doc hadn't actually said *what* schedules these checks at 5 million users — cron jobs, pub/sub, Kafka — and that gap was real. Here's the answer:

**Two trigger shapes → two pieces of infrastructure, not one:**

- **Time-driven (Journeys 2, 5) → cron / batch jobs.** These are calendar-predictable — "due_date − today = 2," "opened_date = 30 days ago." One daily batch query per check, run via a scheduler (Airflow or a simple scheduled cloud function). No per-user job needed; it's a single indexed query across the user base.
- **Event-driven (Journeys 1, 3, 4) → pub/sub.** These fire when upstream data changes — a new bureau pull lands, an AA balance refreshes. The ingesting service (bureau pipeline, AA integration) publishes an event the moment new data arrives; Proactive subscribes to it. This avoids polling millions of records asking "did anything change yet?"

**Why this split, not one tool for both:** the two trigger shapes are fundamentally different problems — "check a calendar" vs. "react the moment something changes" — not a question of user count. At 5M users, this mostly means indexing the batch queries properly and sizing the event topic for message volume, not a fundamentally different architecture than at a much smaller scale.

### Q4. How does Proactive avoid becoming notification spam — what actually governs frequency and timing?

The brief's examples make this look easy — score changed, bill due, utilization high. But if every true condition fires its own notification, a user could get 4-5 messages in one day. Nobody wants that. People mute apps that do this, or just uninstall.

**The simple fix: give each user a daily/weekly limit on how many nudges they can get, no matter how many things are technically true about their account.**

Think of it like a bouncer at a door, not a megaphone that announces everything. A few simple rules do the job:

- **Cap it.** Maybe max 1-2 proactive nudges a day per user, even if 4 things are true.
- **Rank what matters more.** If a user is owed money because their dispute SLA was missed (Journey 5) and *also* happens to have crossed 75% utilization that same day, the money-owed one wins — that's not a "maybe interesting" nudge, that's "GoodScore owes you ₹100/day," it should never lose to a lower-stakes one.
- **Don't repeat yourself if ignored.** If someone dismissed a utilization nudge yesterday, don't show the same one again today — give it a few days' rest before trying that type again.

That's it — a small set of rules sitting between "5 things are true about this user right now" and "what do we actually show them." Nothing fancy, but easy to forget to design on purpose, and it's the difference between Proactive feeling helpful versus feeling like spam.

### Q5. What's the actual cost of this role at GoodScore's scale?

Same discipline applied throughout — checked against the real ~₹4.34 Cr/month revenue baseline, not a theoretical ceiling.

**The case for "cheap by construction":** per Q3, all five journeys are detect-and-template pipelines with **zero model inference cost** — this is a cron/event-scheduling service plus notification delivery, not an AI feature in the conventional sense. The closest thing to "AI" here is Journey 4's trend projection, and that's plain statistics, not a model call.

**Recommendation: Proactive should not be costed as a major standalone line item.** The scheduling/event infrastructure is a standard backend service, not an AI cost center. The one place genuine spend exists is the AA integration itself — there are real, modest per-call costs in AA data-fetch flows industry-wide, plus the FIU registration/compliance overhead — but that's a fixed integration cost, not a per-user variable cost that scales dangerously with MAU.

### Q6. When exactly does a proactive nudge actually surface — mid-session, or only at defined entry points?

**You reframed this one, and it's a cleaner model than the original answer.**

The first pass treated this as a queue-and-resume problem: if a trigger fires while the user is mid-conversation elsewhere, hold it and surface it once they're free. You simplified this: **nudges should only ever appear at the moment the user opens the app, never injected mid-conversation.** There are exactly two surfaces:

1. **Push notification, when the user hasn't opened the app.** The trigger fires in the background (per Q3's logic), and if the user isn't currently in the app, it surfaces as a standard push notification.
2. **In-app nudge, shown at the moment the user opens the app.** If a trigger is pending when the user launches GoodScore, it surfaces right there at open — before any other interaction starts, not during one.

**What this rules out, and why it's the right call:** a trigger never needs to inject itself into an already-active session. Every journey in this doc (score change, bill due, utilization threshold, predicted shortfall, SLA breach) is something the user can just as usefully learn about the next time they open the app as the instant it's detected. This removes an entire category of engineering complexity — no queue-and-resume mechanism, no need to reason about what state an active session was in when a trigger fired. The trigger simply waits at the door: closed app → push notification; app opens → in-app nudge surfaces first. The daily-cap-and-ranking rules from Q4 still decide *which* nudge wins if multiple are pending — it just only ever needs to make that call at one of these two clean entry points.

---

## Summary

**The honest takeaway:** Proactive looks like the "easy, fun" role from the brief's own breezy examples, but its actual hard problem is the opposite of an engineering problem — it's a **restraint problem.** Two findings carry this doc: (1) the brief's own proposed data source for predicting shortfalls (SMS parsing) is very likely non-viable on Play Store policy grounds and should be replaced with the Account Aggregator framework, and (2) deciding *when to stay silent* — capping and ranking nudges so the user isn't flooded, and only ever surfacing them at app-open rather than mid-session — is more architecturally important than any individual trigger.
